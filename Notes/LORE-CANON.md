# Grave Reckoning: The Unquiet Inheritance — canon summary

A working summary of the project owner's full lore document (written 2026-11-16).
The original prose is theirs; this file keeps the facts and the rules that
story text, captions and art must respect. When in doubt, ask them.

> "The grave is a patient thing. It asks no questions of those delivered into
> its keeping. But there are some whom the earth remembers, and others whom it
> refuses to forgive."

## Premise

A young man of great promise, expected to do extraordinary things in life,
instead does something extraordinary in death. He was somebody's son, from an
old family in a land that treats ancestry almost religiously. His death was
preventable; people had the means to stop it and did nothing. The official
account was an accident, and his family accepted it with remarkable speed.
The coffin was lowered, the earth replaced, the grave marked. "The dead,
however, had not agreed to anything."

His vengeance should be understandable without being righteous. Whether those
he seeks deserve their fate is deliberately open.

## The Unquiet Inheritance

An old phenomenon of the land, older than its chapels and mausoleums, now
understood only as superstition. It seems to need three things: a person
descended from an ancient bloodline (**the First Interred**, who according to
tradition once made an agreement with the earth, nature unknown); a death
involving profound betrayal, deliberate or permitted through inaction; and an
unresolved obligation or grievance at burial. Old funeral registers annotate
such names: *Unquiet. Unreconciled. Returned. Account unsettled.* Revenants are
drawn to those associated with their deaths, and they seldom return to the
grave alone.

Folk names for the pursuit and entombment: **the Dance of Death**, or **the
Grave's Measure**. The living contain the revenant; the revenant contains the
living; the grave, indifferent, receives whoever is delivered into its keeping.

## Chapters and who speaks

| Chapter | Place | Speaker | Line |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | The Forgotten Grave | the gravedigger (first witness) | "Nobody dug this one." |
| II | The Old Cemetery | a watchman | "Lanterns up. It's loose." (He says *it*, not *someone*.) |
| III | The Ossuary | the keeper of the ossuary | "We sorted them all. Not this one." |
| IV | The Family Crypt | an elderly undertaker | "The family paid to forget." |
| V | The Chapel Grounds | the senior cleric | "Pray it stays quiet." (spoken toward the sealed chamber beneath the altar) |
| VI | The Ancestral Mausoleum | the mausoleum's keeper, who attended the funeral | "You were never meant to climb out." |

What each chapter reveals, in small pieces only:
- I: the soil pushed up from below, no shovel marks, the headstone tilted. The
  revenant remembers his mother's hands, a promise at a rainy window, voices
  arguing, fear, and someone who was there and turned away. His name is hard to
  recall.
- II: the living recognise him ("it"). Stones obey his touch; those enclosed
  have their lanterns go dark.
- III: his name appears in no register. Past revenants all had an irregular
  burial: altered register, missing witness, coffin sealed without rites, death
  recorded before it happened. Theory: the Inheritance is woken by *concealment*.
- IV: payments to witnesses, an amended physician's account, a dismissed
  servant, an erased family-register entry. Portraits: one resembles his father,
  one resembles him; a name beneath the largest he almost remembers.
- V: chapel manuscripts call revenants *petitioners*. Their grievance can widen
  from the guilty, to the concealers, to anyone who benefited; "the dead might
  cease to distinguish between guilt and association". Something older than the
  church is sealed beneath the altar and has begun to answer.
- VI: the keeper is exhausted, not surprised. The revenant remembers fragments:
  a room, a disagreement, a decision, someone pleading for patience, someone
  insisting there was no other way. And: **the people who buried him knew what
  his bloodline could do, and took precautions to make sure he stayed dead.
  The precautions failed.**

## Rules for story text and art

- **Never depict the young man's death directly.** Funeral, grieving family,
  altered records and reactions are fine. The tragedy stays an absence.
- No explicit violence. Entombed pursuers are not shown dying. What happens to
  them stays unresolved: the living say he kills them; the chapel says the earth
  claims them; the ossuary keeper suspects they are made to live through the
  circumstances of his last moments. No bodies, only extinguished lanterns.
- Keep three questions open: (1) accident or murder, and whether calculated
  inaction differs from murder; (2) whether the revenant is still the young man
  or his grief is being used by something older; (3) why the ancient bloodlines
  were bound to the earth (a covenant, a punishment, or were they its prisoners).
- Keep exposition off the play screens. The six comic chapters stay short and
  mysterious. Depth can live in optional epitaphs, archival illustrations, or an
  unlockable chronicle after the game.

## Ending

After the last pursuer is entombed the revenant reaches the oldest chamber of
the mausoleum: a stone coffin with the family's ancient crest and a worn
inscription. It bears his name, his full *ancestral* name, with a burial date from before
he was born (1864), and an older plate of the same name beyond it (1264).
This has happened before, perhaps many times. He lays a hand on the coffin; something
beneath the stone seems to move; then silence. Fade to a storybook parchment:

> The living had made their peace with his passing.
> The dead had made no such arrangement.

**GRAVE RECKONING** — THE END, OR ANOTHER BEGINNING.

## The cycle (added 2026-11-16, the owner's decision)

The Unquiet Inheritance is a **recurring cycle** that has been happening for
centuries. The revenant is **unaware** of it; the player is shown more than he
sees. The family name is **Aldercross** and the crest is a tree with roots
(crowned shield, two lions). The coffin in the final chamber bears
**E. ALDERCROSS** with a burial date of **MDCCCLXIV (1864)**, a generation before the
revenant's own time; a second image shows the same plate reading
**MCCLXIV (1264)**. The ending shows both in turn, with the narrator (not the
revenant) noting "The same name. The same crest. Another date, centuries older.
He did not look closer." Earlier evidence already in the lore supports it: the
ossuary keeper's registers of past revenants with irregular burials; the
Chapter VI precautions that "had failed" this time. Keep it implicit; never
have a character state that it is a cycle.

## Reconciling the game's mechanics with this lore

- **Sealing a pursuer** is the Grave's Measure: they are delivered into the
  grave's keeping by the stones. The coffin sprite is that moment. Their lantern
  goes dark. What becomes of them is not stated.
- **All pursuers must be sealed together** before the flames rise: the measure
  is complete only when no walker is left.
- **The blue flame pickup** is not a claim about the victims. Treat it as a
  flicker left where a lantern went out, something the revenant gathers; the
  lore lets it stay ambiguous (a fragment of his own memory, or the grave's
  keeping). Do not state what it is beyond what the lore supports.
- **A pursuer who walks into an open grave** is received by the grave but not
  delivered by the revenant: no flame.
- **Humans are fallible**: attention spans fail, they stumble into open graves.

## Voices: who calls him what (added 2026-11-16)

The living speak AT him and he never answers. Each one's pronoun and tone follow their
relationship to him, and the plates (`say` / `speaker` / `at` in story.txt) must keep to it.

| Speaker | Calls him | Manner |
|---|---|---|
| The sexton (I) | nothing: "this one" (the grave) | A working man alone at night, bewildered; speaks half to himself, plain words. |
| The watch (II) | **"it"**, never someone or something | Orders shouted to men; recognition he will not admit; officialdom handling a thing. |
| The keeper of the bones (III) | "this one", as an entry missing from his registers | Whispers; troubled more by the gap in the book than by the creature; a scholar's phrasing. |
| The undertaker (IV) | "the family", never "you" | Elderly, defensive and ashamed; professional euphemism ("paid", "discretion"); speaks in the third person. |
| The senior cleric (V) | does not address him at all | Speaks toward the sealed chamber under the altar; **"it"**; prayer, not conversation. |
| The mausoleum's keeper (VI) | **"you"**: the only one who does | Knew him in life; exhausted, not surprised; quiet, direct, past fear. |

So the second person is held back for the very last living voice. Nobody before the keeper
says "you" to him, and nobody names him.

## Journal voices (added 2026-11-16)

48 pages (37 in the casebook, 11 earlier hands). Printed pages are documents with unnamed or institutional authors (a testimonial,
a physician's account, a vestry inventory); never name the investigator or the young man.
New pages keep the lore's three open questions open: the physician's struck line says
"neglect, and want of timely help" (preventable, never how); the First Interred paper offers
covenant, penance and prison without choosing; the lantern note gives the Watch's, the
Reverend's and the keeper's three opinions without a fourth.

## The book and the earlier hands (added 2026-11-16)

The Journal is a community's book: lent, never given, from a teacher who asks after the dead of
the district to the protege who takes it on (the player). Its flyleaf says so ("I had it from my
teacher, as you have it from me... Add your pages, and not your name."). Earlier keepers bound in
what they found. Nobody is named; the investigator, the teacher and every earlier hand stay
unnamed. Nobody says it is a cycle.

| Year | Hand | Takes it for | Pages (deed that loosens them) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1264 | A monastery chronicle, copied in by a later keeper for one marginal word ("Unquiet") | a distant atrocity with many hands in it, most irrelevant (the lord, the abbot, the reeve, the steward, hired spearmen, and the brethren of the Temple, who "kept their gates shut and heard nothing"; a Hospitaller who brought salves and asked no questions; a Teutonic brother bound for Prussia who lodged one night and was gone before matins: nods to real orders, no more. Cathars deliberately left out: persecuted by a crusade rather than part of one, and no real presence in an English-style vale, so a mention would imply a link to the duel page) | Troubles in the Vale (lanterns 40); Burying at the Ford (offerings 20) |
| 1587 | A clerk's roll; a physician | the Adversary, then witchcraft; "this desires only to be answered" | Deposition (a pursuer stumbles into a grave); Physician to His Friend (6 stumbles); Last Leaf (3 flawless graves) |
| 1741 | A learned paper; a catalogue; a letter, unsent | a distemper of the blood; "a conclusion is being drawn" | Observations (a company of 3 laid to rest together); Catalogue (60 offerings); Letter (the way proved shut once) |
| 1864 | The keeper before ours | an inquiry; "the registers are in order, which is the first thing wrong with them" | Notes (10 flawless graves); Witnesses (2 chapters settled on the hardest vigil); Last Entry (4 chapters on the hardest vigil) |

The 1264 leaf is deliberately distant: the lore's four register years are the only thread. It
says nothing about the young man or the family, so the 1264 plate in the ending stays the
player's discovery. The 1864 keeper goes down to the oldest chamber too; the hint to read it as
the same chamber is the player's.

## Evidence and inheritance (the owner's principle, 2026-11-16)

The investigators try to report on evidence, but across centuries the line between what is
plainly evidence (objective) and what only seems to be (subjective) blurs into interpretation.
What the book teaches is that **someone thought it was important**; how much it matters now is
left for the reader to decide. So the earlier pages never say what a detail proves. Where it
helps, a later keeper's line in another hand says only why the page was kept ("Kept on the word
of the keeper before me, who thought it mattered. I cannot say what it proves.").

Applied so far: the 1587 deposition now has a wandering preacher "of the old southern sort,
whom the vicar calls a heretic", lodged in the parish that week and seen at the old end at dusk,
since gone. He is a loose thread on purpose: witness, cause, bystander or nothing. (A nod to the
Cathars as a name for the feeling of it, nothing stated.) Do not resolve him.

Page space: a journal page holds about eleven lines of body text above the margin marks. The
text audit does not catch body text running under the footer, so check long pages by eye.

## Margins: the book's other hands (added 2026-11-16)

The book has passed through several keepers, and some argued with what they read. Three hands
(`margin <hand> <words>` in journal.txt; ink, size and tilt differ):

| Hand | Looks like | Temperament |
|---|---|---|
| teacher | blue-black ink, brisk, rises to the right | corrects the method: "Who SET the stone? Paying is another question." / "He promised. So did you." |
| elder | faded brown, small, sags | has been where the reader is going: "I looked. Do not." / "No date. Who closed the book?" |
| pencil | grey, level | the sceptic, offering the ordinary explanation: "Frost heave. Look at the date." / "Coin proves a bribe. Not a motive." |

Rules: questions and objections only, never answers; the sceptic is allowed to be right about
small things and the elder is never allowed to explain. None of the notes names anyone. They ask
the reader what the keeper before them wondered: provenance ("Unsent. Then how did we come by
it?"), motive, rehearsal, or whether the evidence is evidence. 15 notes so far, about one page
in three. A page that is already full drops its notes (the text audit prints `TEXT-NOTE`); on a
page that is nearly full a note tucks in to the right of the tally marks.

## Wear (added 2026-11-16)

The book is old and shows it. `((words))` in journal.txt means worn away: the words are gone, with
a faint tea-coloured stain and a gap the length of the words. 13 gaps so far, hand-picked, one page
in four or so, all on documents and none on the investigator's own pages (fresh ink). Pick words
the page can lose without losing its point (a dusk, a stone, a name), and never a word another
hand's margin note quotes. Examples: "barred from without and ((fired))", "I shall leave off when
I have the ((name))", "the old ((southern)) sort".

## Why the investigator measures what the revenant does (added 2026-11-16)

The player is the revenant, and the Journal is the investigator's. The bridge: **the book shows you.**
Each grave is a vigil the book makes the investigator live (the "Sleep" note already says they dream
rooms they have never stood in and that he is "always one stone ahead of me"; the last note is "I am
going down. Not alone."). The Journal is the waking record of what they saw. The Record of the Vigils
is where the two meet: the investigator's own count of the revenant's deeds, in their hand. Progress
costs something, which is why the handwriting frays as the numbers grow. By the end "the count is
the book's now". Do not explain this on the play screens: it lives in the flyleaf, the Record, the
"Entered in the book." line and the investigator's own notes, and stays a feeling, not a rule.
